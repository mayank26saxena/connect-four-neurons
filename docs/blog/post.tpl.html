Interpretability · research note

Neurons are a good basis for some concepts and a poor one for others

Goodfire argued that the raw neurons of a language model are a usable unit of analysis. We tested that in a small transformer that plays Connect Four, where every concept has an exact answer. The picture is more mixed, and a wider layer made it worse.

In short

  1. {{fam_line_n}} vs {{fam_line_r}}open-line poles matching a concept: neurons vs random directions
    Neurons are a good basis for local concepts such as lines, landing cells and threat cells. For a global one, a full column, random directions do better ({{fam_full_n}} vs {{fam_full_r}}).
  2. {{gap256}} → {{gap4096}}points, neurons minus random directions, 256 → 4,096 neurons per layer
    We expected wider layers to make neurons cleaner. The opposite happened at constant playing strength, and all three predictions we wrote down beforehand failed.
  3. {{blk_off20}} vs {{blk_rand20}}blocking rate: 20 concept neurons off vs 20 random neurons off
    Switching off the 20 neurons that best track "must block" cuts blocking from {{blk_intact}}. Twenty random neurons change nothing. One neuron alone barely matters: the job is spread over tens.

Interpretability work needs a unit to describe: a feature, a direction, a neuron. Sparse autoencoders learn new units because single neurons are thought to mix several meanings. Tigges et al. (Goodfire, 2026) argue that the raw neurons of a large mixture-of-experts model are interpretable after all: they are functionally sparse, each pole has few modes of computation, and written descriptions predict when they fire. Their checks relied on language models judging descriptions of text, so there was no exact answer to compare against. We asked the same questions in a model where there is one.

01What we built

The game is Connect Four. A search engine, the teacher, looks {{depth}} moves ahead and scores each of the seven columns. We generated {{games_train}} games and trained a {{params}}-parameter transformer to predict the teacher's scores from the move sequence alone, so it has to build its own picture of the board. Its MLP layers use the same bias-free SwiGLU design as the model Goodfire studied: six layers and {{neurons_main}} neurons. It picks one of the teacher's best moves at {{agree_main}} of positions, takes {{take_win}} of the winning moves on offer, blocks {{block_rate}} of threats, and beats its own depth-10 teacher head to head ({{win_d10}} wins, {{draw_d10}} draws, {{loss_d10}} losses over 98 games).

A rules engine then labels every position with {{n_concepts}} exact concepts: who holds each cell, where the next stone in each column lands, which columns are full, which cell would complete four for either side, forced blocks and wins, and open twos and threes on each of the 69 four-cell lines. This is what lets us check a neuron against the truth rather than against a judge.

02Can most activations be removed?

Goodfire's first claim is functional sparsity: in their model, zeroing the weakest {{gf_ablated}} of activations at every step cost {{gf_cost}} on likelihood benchmarks. We zero everything inside each neuron's central band and check how often the model still picks one of the teacher's best moves. The metrics differ, so the numbers are not directly comparable, but the contrast is clear.

Our model is not sparse in this sense: zeroing the central 90% costs {{cost_p5}} points. Magnitude still matters. Keeping only the 64 largest of 1,024 neurons at each layer and position keeps {{agree_top64}} agreement, against {{agree_rand64}} for 64 random ones. Strong firings are mostly gate-positive: {{gate_pos_firing}} of firings against {{gate_pos_all}} of all activations.

03Do neurons line up with concepts?

The second claim is that neurons are interpretable units. Averaged over every concept, ours are no better than random directions: {{share_n}} of neuron poles match a concept, against {{share_r}} of random directions. The average hides a split.

One reason for the split: a global feature such as "the game is nearly over" is a direction across many neurons, so a random mix of all of them captures it more often than any single neuron does. The two poles of one neuron are only weakly related: their best concepts fall in the same family {{pi_fam}} of the time, against {{pi_fam_sh}} for randomly paired poles.

04Does a wider layer make neurons cleaner?

Goodfire's model has {{gf_neurons}} neurons; ours has {{neurons_main}}. A natural explanation for the mismatch is scale: a layer with more neurons than it needs has less pressure to pack several meanings into each. We wrote down three predictions before running anything, then trained models with 256 to 4,096 neurons per layer, two training seeds each, and everything else fixed.

Playing strength barely moved ({{agree_w256}} to {{agree_w4096}} agreement), so this is not a skill effect. Wider layers seem to spread each function over more neurons. That says nothing directly about a model hundreds of times wider, trained on language, so Goodfire's result is not contradicted. But extrapolating our trend would point away from it, not toward it.

05Do these neurons do anything?

Matching a concept does not show that the model uses the neuron. We tested this on {{n_action}} neurons matched to a win, a block or a poisoned column. Switching off one neuron changes the matching behaviour by a median of {{single_med}} points. So we ranked all neurons by how well they match one concept, and switched off the top k together, against k random neurons from the same layers.

So the neurons that track a concept do cause the behaviour, but the work is shared across tens of them. For describing what the model does, a single neuron is not the unit of function here, even where it is the unit of a concept.

06Which comes first, the behaviour or the neuron?

We saved {{n_ckpt}} checkpoints during training and repeated the matching test at each.

The behaviour is built first, from distributed circuitry. Neurons then become more aligned with it over thousands more steps. A neuron-level description read off the final model would therefore describe a late, partial summary of how the behaviour arises.

07Limits, and what's next

  • One game at small scale. Everything here is a 3M to 20M-parameter model on Connect Four. It shows that a result can depend on width and on the kind of concept; it says little about language models.
  • Our vocabulary and our modes. A pole may track something we did not label. Modes are our own construction (exact input attributions clustered, then merged by concept profile), not an LLM judge's, and are soft: in the main model, {{top90}} of sampled poles have one dominant mode, and {{top90_feat}} of the best concept-matched ones, against {{gf_modes}} reported by Goodfire.
  • The model is strong, not perfect. {{agree_main}} is agreement with a depth-10 search. We have not yet measured optimal play with an exact solver.
  • The SAE comparison is inconclusive (Appendix B).

Next: measure optimality with an exact solver, train a properly tuned SAE, and vary the teacher's strength at a fixed width, to see whether these results depend on how well the model plays.

Appendix B: neurons against a sparse autoencoder

For each layer we trained a TopK sparse autoencoder (Gao et al. 2024) with 4,096 latents and k = 32 on neuron activations, then scored its latents with the same F1 procedure. It is under-trained: {{sae_dead}} of its {{sae_total}} latents fire on fewer than 0.5% of positions and are excluded, and the unexplained variance is {{sae_fvu_min}} in the best layer and {{sae_fvu_max}} in the worst. Over the {{sae_live}} live latents, {{sae_p97}} match a concept at the 97th-percentile threshold, against {{nrn_p97}} of neuron poles; over the threshold sweep it is {{sae_sw}} against {{nrn_sw}}. The two criteria disagree, so we draw no conclusion before a properly tuned dictionary (dead-latent resampling, longer training, a width and k sweep).

Appendix C: how modes were measured

For each sampled pole we took up to 2,000 firings and decomposed the neuron's gate and up inputs exactly into contributions from the embeddings, every attention head and every earlier neuron (checked to sum to the true pre-activation). Firings were clustered with Leiden community detection on a cosine nearest-neighbour graph of those contributions, and clusters whose concept-rate profiles correlate above 0.8 were merged into modes. Each firing's damage is the KL divergence it causes when that neuron is zeroed at that one position, summed over the rest of the game. In the main model the median pole has {{modes_median}} modes, {{modes_single}} have exactly one, and for multi-mode poles the median share of damage outside the main mode is {{modes_outside}}. Adding the 276 line concepts to the vocabulary moved the one-dominant-mode share, which is why we treat mode counts as soft.

Appendix D: reproducing this, and what went wrong on the way
  • Data are a pure function of the config: the teacher returns exact depth-limited minimax scores, move sampling uses a seeded generator, and the softmax avoids the C library's exp so results do not depend on the platform. Every output has a .meta.json with the source hash, config, environment and output hash.
  • Training, all analyses and the sweep ran on rented GPUs for under $3 in total. A GPU memory error at 2,048 neurons per layer interrupted the sweep once; the fix processes neurons in blocks and a test checks it matches the unblocked code.
  • An early version of the match metric searched firing thresholds down to the median, which let every unit match something, random directions included. We restricted it to the tail (90th to 99.5th percentile) before any main result.
  • {{n_e_fail}} of our {{n_e_total}} pre-registered expectations for the main model failed (Appendix A). We report them as failed.
