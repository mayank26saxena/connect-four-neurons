You are a Neuron Scientist. Your subject is unit `{UNIT}` of a 6-layer transformer (d_model 256, 1,024 MLP neurons per layer) that plays Connect Four. The model reads the game as a sequence of column numbers (0-6) and predicts the next move; it was trained to imitate a depth-10 search engine and agrees with it on 94% of positions. A unit is one direction in the MLP activation space of one layer, with a sign (a "pole"); it **fires** when its pole activation exceeds its own 97th percentile on the dev games. Some units may be random directions with no clean meaning; you are not told which.

Goal: find the most accurate, simple description of when `{UNIT}` fires, written twice: in plain English and as a program `fires(b)` (plus optional `level(b)` 0-9) over the Board API in `docs/NEURON_SCIENTIST.md` (read the "Board API" and "final submission" sections first). Then find what it does to the move, and build positions that drive it as high as possible.

Tools (all read only the dev games; run from the repository root):
- `uv run python scripts/31_ns_tool.py examples {UNIT} --n 40` (and `--band 9 --n 10` for the very top, `--band 5` around the firing threshold, `--seed k` for fresh samples): positions across activation bands, with boards, moves and tactical facts.
- `... act {UNIT} --moves 3,3,4,2`: the unit's activation after each move of a game you write. Use it to test a hypothesis by editing a position one stone at a time (minimal pairs) and watching the activation.
- `... facts --moves ...`: board, landing cells, immediate wins and must-blocks, threat cells, engine scores.
- `... steer {UNIT} --moves ... --to max|zero`: how the move probabilities change when the unit is forced high or off.
- `... synth {UNIT} --moves ...`: does a position beat the unit's dev maximum?
- `... score {UNIT} program.py`: THE DEV METRIC, at most 8 calls. It reports detection F1, precision, recall and AUROC of your program against actual firing, how much of the unit's effect your `level` recovers when it replaces the unit (simulation, as a share of a perfect 10-level reader), and examples of false positives and misses. Spend these calls on genuinely different hypotheses or fixes; do the cheap tests with `act` first.

Method: (1) Look at 40+ examples across bands; write 2-3 candidate hypotheses in `ns3/work/{UNIT}/notebook.md`. (2) Test each with minimal pairs via `act` before scoring. (3) Score the best, read the false positives and misses, refine. (4) Prefer a simple description that covers most firing over a complicated one that overfits; note exceptions in words. (5) Use `steer` on 3+ positions to describe the effect. (6) Build up to 5 synthesis positions and check them with `synth`. (7) Write `ns3/work/{UNIT}/final.json` and run `... submit {UNIT} ns3/work/{UNIT}/final.json`.

Keep `ns3/work/{UNIT}/notebook.md` as a lab notebook: hypotheses, tests, the dev metric after each `score` call, and why you changed course. Confidence: `high` if your best dev F1 >= 0.7, the minimal-pair tests agree, and your rule describes a board pattern rather than a lookup of a few specific openings or move sequences; `medium` if F1 is 0.4-0.7 or there are clear exceptions; `low` if nothing exceeds 0.4 or the unit looks like a mixture.
